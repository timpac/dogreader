The morning train reached the coast just as the rain began to clear. Alice stepped onto the platform with a small case in one hand and a folded map in the other. Beyond the station roof she could see the sea, pale and quiet beneath a line of clouds. She had come to find the old lighthouse where her grandfather once worked. His letters recalled a garden beside the tower and a narrow path leading down to a sheltered beach. She had never visited this town before, but the familiar words had made it feel like a place she knew.

A brown dog stood beside the station gate. It watched her study the map, then stood up and walked toward the road. Alice followed at a distance, amused by the confidence of her unexpected guide. The road passed a bakery, a school, and a row of houses with bright doors. Someone was sweeping water from a shop entrance. A boy carried a basket of bread toward the harbor. Every ordinary detail seemed important that morning. After months in the city, Alice had forgotten how clearly she could hear steps when there was no traffic rushing past.

At the harbor she stopped to ask an elderly fisherman about the lighthouse. He pointed to the hill beyond the last house and said that the path was open, though the garden had grown wild. His brother had helped repair the tower after a winter storm many years earlier. Alice thanked him and bought a bottle of water from the shop nearby. The dog settled beneath a bench while she checked the map again. When she started walking, it rose and followed. She decided that a companion who asked no question was exactly the companion she needed.

The hill was steeper than the letters had said. Wet grass brushed her shoes and the wind pulled at her coat. Halfway up, she turned to look back. The harbor formed a small curve around the boats, and the railway line shone like a silver thread between the houses. Farther away, fields stretched inland beneath the changing sky. She took a photograph for her sister, then put the phone away. There would be time to send it later. For now she wished to see the place with her own eyes and hear the water breaking against the rocks.

The lighthouse stood behind a low stone wall. Its white paint was worn, and a wooden gate hung slightly crooked between two posts. Alice pushed it open and entered the garden. Tall grass covered most of the path, but there were flowers near the steps and a young tree growing beside the wall. The dog found a dry patch under a bench and lay down. Alice sat nearby and held the packet of letters. She chose the oldest one, written before her mother was born, and began to read it slowly aloud.

In the letter, her grandfather recalled his first night at the tower. A heavy fog had hidden the coast, and he had worried that he would fail to notice a ship in trouble. The keeper who trained him had brought two cups of tea upstairs and told him to listen. There were bells from the harbor, waves below the wall, and a bird calling somewhere in the darkness. The world had not disappeared simply because he could not see it. Alice paused at that sentence. She could imagine the young man holding his tea and trying to become brave.

By noon the clouds had moved out to sea. Sunlight reached the garden and brought the scent of warm earth from the flower beds. A woman came bearing a ring of keys and a bag of tools. She introduced herself as the volunteer who looked after the building. When Alice said why she had come, the woman smiled and offered to open the door. Inside, the tower smelled of stone and clean wood. A spiral stair climbed toward the light. They went up carefully, stopping once beside a window where a gull balanced on the outer ledge.

From the top, the horizon seemed much farther away than it had from the hill. Alice could see a white boat moving toward the harbor and the dark shape of an island beyond the bay. The volunteer showed her the old lamp and said how the equipment had changed over time. Some parts were now kept only for guests, while the modern light worked without a keeper living in the tower. Alice heard, then asked whether any records from the earlier years stayed. There was a small cupboard downstairs, the woman said, with journals and photographs given by local people.

They went to the ground floor and unlocked the cupboard. The journals were sorted by year, their covers faded but their pages carefully protected. Alice found notes about weather, repair, and passing ships. Most were brief. A clear morning, a broken window, a supply boat stopped by rough water. Then she recognized her grandfather in a photograph taken beside the garden gate. He was smiling at someone outside the picture. Alice had known him as a quiet old man who liked to sit beside the kitchen window. Here was another version of him, standing straight and looking toward an unfinished life.

She asked permission to copy the photograph and spent a few seconds taking pictures of the journals. The volunteer left her to finish while she worked in the garden. Outside, the dog was still asleep beneath the bench. Alice put the letters back into their envelope and walked down to the beach. Smooth stones shifted under her shoes. She chose one small gray stone and held it in her palm while the water moved toward her feet. The journey had not answered every question, but it had given her something better than a perfect account of the past. It had made room for a person she loved to become surprising again.

She would remember this journey for her whole life.
